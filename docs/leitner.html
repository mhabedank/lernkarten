lernkarten back to the start

the method · Sebastian Leitner, 1972

The box does the scheduling

Three or four compartments, one rule. A card you get right moves forward and comes back less often; a card you get wrong goes back to the first. There is no app, no account and nothing to sync — the card's position is its state, and the whole system keeps working during a power cut.

01One session, five moves

A session is one compartment, front to back, ten minutes on most days. The box tells you which one, so you never spend the first five minutes deciding what to study.

  1. 1
    Take the compartment that is due.

    Compartment 1 every day; the others on their own day, from the table below.

  2. 2
    Answer out loud before you turn the card.

    Out loud, or on paper. “I would have known it” is not knowing it.

  3. 3
    Right? One compartment forward.

    Fully right, without peeking. Behind the next divider it goes — you will see it again, later rather than tomorrow.

  4. 4
    Wrong? All the way back to the first.

    Back to compartment 1, not one step back. Read the answer once, then bury the card in the middle of the pile rather than on top of it.

  5. 5
    Compartment empty? The session is over.

    Do not “just do one more”. The gaps between sessions are not a side effect of the method; they are the method.

02When each compartment is due

The interval is printed on every divider, so the box explains itself with the lid open. Pick days rather than gaps — “every Tuesday and Friday” is something you can keep; “every second day” is something you have to remember.

Four compartments — the fuller version.
CompartmentDueWhat is in it
1dailyEverything new, and everything you got wrong. Kept small on purpose: if it overflows, you are adding cards faster than you are learning them.
2every 2 daysSurvived one round.
3weeklyThe same weekday each week.
4every 2 weeksThe exit. A card that is right here has earned its way out of the box; one that is wrong goes back to the first, no exceptions.
Three compartments — fewer sessions, not shorter gaps.
CompartmentDueWhat is in it
1dailyNew and wrong cards.
2every 3 daysSurvived one round.
3every 2 weeksThe exit.

Both sets end at the same longest rest, and that is deliberate. Choosing three compartments should cost you sessions, not spacing — what the far end of the box is for does not change because you decided to keep fewer piles.

03Printing it

The dividers come out of the same build as the cards, on the same paper, cut from the same sheets. They usually fill space the last sheet was wasting. 

lernkarten build cards/*.yaml --grid a8 --dividers 4

Cut along the line drawn on the divider, not along the colour. The colour deliberately runs past the edge so that a slightly crooked cut still leaves colour at the border; the line is where the divider actually ends.

You will want somewhere to put them: the card box is one A4 sheet to cut, fold and glue. Print it on 160–250 gsm — heavier paper than the cards want, which is why it comes as its own file. It fits a deck printed at --grid a8.

Every freshly cut card starts in compartment 1. Do not backfill: a card you are sure of still has to earn its way forward, one right answer at a time.

04Four house rules

The box only works if the moves are honest. These are the four places every paper system quietly dies.

Never write on a card

No compartment numbers, no tally marks. The card moves; anything written on it is wrong the first time it does. The position carries the state so the card does not have to.

No tracking file, no app

The moment the schedule exists in two places they disagree — and the file is the one that will be wrong. The box is the single source of truth.

Reprint a topic, not the deck

Cutting a fresh full deck throws away everything the box was tracking. Fixed one card? Reprint that topic and swap the cards in place, into the compartments they came from.

Decide what “done” means

A card that passes the last compartment can retire into an envelope, or go back to the front. Leitner has no answer and neither does this page — but a box that never empties grows without bound, so pick one.

05Why it works, and what it is not

Recalling something just before you would have forgotten it is what makes it stick. Growing intervals put every card near that moment, and the cards you struggle with automatically get the most repetitions — you never have to decide which those are.

Leitner's own box had no intervals at all. In So lernt man lernen (1972) the compartments are 1, 2, 5, 8 and 14 cm wide, and you work through one when it is full. The spacing comes from capacity: a wider compartment takes longer to fill, so the gap grows by itself, with no calendar anywhere.

That needs a bigger box than one A4 sheet folds into — at 24 mm of depth the first compartment would hold five cards. So the intervals above are a calendar simplification of his idea rather than his idea, and it seemed better to say so than to put his name on a schedule he never wrote.
